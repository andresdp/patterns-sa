# ADEPT framework context

## What ADEPT does

ADEPT is a data-driven framework that connects an explicit system model to observed configurations, operating conditions, and quality outcomes. It characterizes the observed design space, identifies parameters useful for understanding outcome variation, and uses scenario discovery—such as PRIM and CART—to find joint decision/context constraints that predict membership in a target outcome region. The resulting boxes help architects inspect candidate prescriptions and operating envelopes and assess how reliably and broadly the rule identifies the target in held-out observations.

Scenario discovery can be predictive while the analysis remains observational. A predictive box can inform architecture choices and motivate causal hypotheses for later testing; it does not establish a causal mechanism or guarantee behavior beyond the evaluated data.

## Diagnose the two primary result artefacts

These guidelines apply across application domains. Translate the generic terms below to the current system model: a **configuration** is a complete assignment of design decisions/policies; **context** is the set of other recorded conditions under which an observation occurred; **outcomes** are measured quality attributes; a **tradeoff/region** is a defined category or area in outcome space; and a **box** is a conjunction of feature conditions selected by a discovery method.

### 1. Objective space, regions, and configuration allocation

Use the objective-space chart together with its underlying tables. The chart shows where observed runs lie in outcome space; cells or regions summarize those outcomes; allocation tables show which configurations and other recorded factors appear in each region.

Interpret in this order:

1. **Establish preference before inspecting occupancy.** Check each measured outcome's meaning, unit, transformations, and direction of preference using domain semantics or explicit stakeholder goals. Define the preferred or acceptable joint region(s) from those directions and the study's bin boundaries. Region labels identify ranges, not ratings. Do not let the observed points determine which outcomes are desirable.
2. **Then report actual occupancy.** For the selected scope, state the denominator, count/share in every region (including zeros), and distribution across populated regions. Keep the semantic target distinct from observed occupancy. If a preferred region is empty, report it as desired but unobserved; do not silently replace it with an occupied region. Empty means unobserved in this dataset and scope, not infeasible. Compare counts/proportions rather than point density alone.
3. **Trace allocation at the right level.** Compare complete configurations within the same analysis scope, then describe how observations are allocated among outcome regions. Keep a complete decision assignment distinct from marginal summaries such as the number of active options, individual feature frequencies, or instance counts. Marginal groups can combine different configurations and contexts.
4. **Separate design from context.** A region's allocation may vary with both chosen policies/configurations and non-controlled or sampled context. Describe these as observed associations. If the evidence does not isolate a policy from context, do not attribute the allocation to that policy alone.
5. **Diagnose support and comparability.** Check denominators, rare configurations, missing combinations, sampling/split structure, and whether compared groups share the same outcome definitions and population. Identify when a difference could reflect composition or imbalance. Treat changes across slices as contrasts between observed slices unless the design supports a stronger comparison.
6. **Translate the pattern into a decision-relevant next step.** Say which alternatives or operating contexts deserve further exploration, which region lacks evidence, and what additional observations or controlled comparison would resolve uncertainty. Do not turn descriptive occupancy into a guarantee or feasibility boundary.

An objective-space conclusion should therefore name: (a) metric semantics and preference directions, (b) the preferred/acceptable region(s) and whether each is occupied, (c) region count/share and denominator, (d) which complete configurations are represented or over/under-represented, (e) relevant context differences, and (f) limits on attribution. Contingency measures may supplement counts and within-group proportions when support permits; state the measure and denominator and do not treat association as causation. When a point-level plot and allocation table appear inconsistent, check aggregation, filtering, and labels before interpreting either.

### 2. Discovery boxes and roles of relevant features

A discovered box is a joint rule over decisions and context intended to predict membership in a specified target region. Read all conditions together. A box is not a list of independent effects, a causal model, or evidence that the rule has already been imposed on the architecture.

Diagnose each box with these checks:

1. **Carry forward the RQ1 target.** State why the region is preferred or acceptable from outcome semantics, whether it is occupied in this notebook, its source population, discovery method, partition, and support. Do not switch to a more populated region without an explicit domain/stakeholder rationale. Clarify whether the rule was fitted on one partition and evaluated on another; never label metrics by their most recent evaluation if both partitions matter.
2. **Classify feature roles using the system model.** Separate decision/policy variables (choices an architect can set) from context or operating conditions (conditions that vary around the design), outcome-derived variables, identifiers, and other recorded descriptors. Use declared relationships to explain how a low-level condition relates to its parent decision. This adds syntactic context, not a causal pathway. If a feature's role is ambiguous, say so rather than inferring it from its name.
3. **Explain the whole conjunction.** Translate thresholds and categories faithfully, preserving units, inclusivity, category meaning, missing/not-selected states, and parent-decision implications. Group conditions into design prescription and operating/context envelope when the model supports that distinction. Explicitly note when the box does not constrain a decision or permits multiple decision states.
4. **Explain feature importance separately from box conditions.** Importance ranks indicate predictive credit within a particular fitted model and slice. They do not provide direction, a threshold, a causal effect, or proof that a highly ranked feature belongs in every box. Check consistency across relevant slices and flag instability, correlated proxies, constant or near-constant features, zero importance, and omitted expected parameters.
5. **Assess prediction, target capture, and coverage together.** For discovery and held-out partitions separately, report target prevalence, precision, target recall/coverage, lift relative to that partition's prevalence, and rows retained. Precision and recall together describe how pure and how complete target identification is. High precision with low coverage is a narrow candidate prescription; high coverage with low precision admits many non-target rows. Discovery performance that degrades on held-out rows indicates limited predictive stability. Small support makes rates uncertain.
6. **Interpret held-out evaluation as predictive architectural evidence.** Applying the fixed rule to held-out observations tests whether joint conditions continue to identify the target among runs not used for discovery. Compare with the unrestricted baseline from the same partition and, when useful, an explicitly named configuration. This supports within-campaign predictive guidance, not proof that imposing the rule causes the outcome. Claim reduced continuous-outcome variance only when dispersion is directly measured and compared; discrete target metrics alone do not show it.
7. **Crosscheck the candidate rule with domain knowledge.** Assess whether the joint conditions are consistent with, mixed relative to, or not informative about known mechanisms or expert expectations. Such agreement can motivate causal hypotheses but is not causal validation. Similar rules across algorithms or partitions strengthen predictive consistency; different rules can reflect method objectives, correlated predictors, or limited support. No box means the method did not identify a rule under the chosen setup, not that the target is impossible.
8. **End with conditional architectural guidance.** Describe a candidate design prescription and operating envelope, how well it predicts the target, its support and coverage cost, and the next validation needed. Avoid guarantees beyond the stated scope.

For every reported box, make clear **what is a decision**, **what is context**, **which semantically selected target it predicts**, **how well it predicts and captures the target**, and **how much support remains**. Report feature importance as a separate candidate-screening signal, not as an effect estimate. Use predictive language for validated rules (“the rule identifies/predicts the target in held-out observations”) and observational language for associations. Causal explanations may be proposed as hypotheses, but must be labelled for follow-up validation unless an independent causal design supports them.

## Interpretation for this analysis

- A configuration is a joint assignment of design decisions/policies; a run is one observed execution under that configuration and particular context/conditions.
- Feature importance is model-relative predictive credit, not effect direction or causal influence. A PRIM/CART box is a joint predictive rule over design and operating conditions.
- Held-out evaluation tests whether the fixed rule predicts target membership on observations not used for discovery. It supports within-campaign predictive guidance, but does not impose the rule on a live system, estimate a causal intervention, or guarantee future behavior. Report prevalence, precision, recall/coverage, lift where available, and retained support together.
- Robustness-oriented guidance may be motivated by a rule that identifies a desired tradeoff under stated conditions. Discrete target metrics do not establish reduced variance in continuous outcomes; that requires direct dispersion evidence.
For campaign-specific outcome semantics, decisions, operating conditions, and theory-informed expectations, see the [Federated Learning domain reference](federated-learning-domain.md) and the relevant study README. These domain-specific definitions do not generalize automatically to other ADEPT studies.
